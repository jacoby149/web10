\title{web10: User-Owned Social Data with IAM-Grade Access Contracts \\ at Zero Scan Penalty}
\author{Jacob Hoffman}
\affiliation{\institution{web10 Inc.}\country{}}
\email{jacob@web10.app}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
User-owned-data protocols such as Solid and ActivityPub defined the idea---
your data, your rules---but stopped at the specification: container-level
access control, no group/role model, and storage that cannot run a real social
feed. They produced a protocol without a product.

We present \textbf{web10}, a next-generation user-owned-data protocol \emph{and}
its execution: a running social platform (feeds, groups, direct messages,
discovery) built end to end on a modern columnar data-lakehouse backend. Access
is governed by fine-grained, revocable, group/role-scoped contracts---cloud IAM
primitives brought to the \emph{front} of the user---enforced at the query layer
so fine-grained authorization costs an index lookup, not a scan.

The application is the research. In a single query-engine call, web10 returns a
full feed of \textbf{260 assets}, each access-checked against the reader's role
grants: a large, tunable feed and fine-grained user-gated access coexisting in
one query. That conjunction is what prior systems proposed but could not ship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The dominant social platforms solve engagement extremely well and ownership
extremely poorly. A user's posts, messages, and relationships live in the
platform's database under the platform's access model. The user can adjust a
handful of visibility settings the platform chose to expose; they cannot
redefine who a ``friend'' or a ``member'' is, cannot contract access
per-service, and cannot take their data---let alone their audience---
elsewhere. When the platform changes its rules or goes away, the user's data
goes with it.

Cloud infrastructure solved the authorization half of this problem long ago.
AWS IAM and its descendants give operators fine-grained, role-based,
service-scoped, revocable access control with cryptographic token
verification. But that model lives at the \emph{infrastructure} boundary: the
cloud provider owns the data plane, and the end user is invisible to it.
Nobody has brought the IAM model to the \emph{user} boundary of a social
platform---where the user, not the platform, is the principal that holds the
contracts.

Every serious attempt at user-owned social data has stopped at the same wall,
and the gaps are specific. \textbf{Solid} gives users personal data stores
(Pods) and a body of work on building social applications over them, but its
access control is WebACL---permissions attached to \emph{containers}, with no
first-class group/role primitive and no way for a person to request, get
approved, and receive a per-service permission set; and its per-Pod storage
makes a cross-user feed query intractable, so those social applications remain
hypothetical. \textbf{ActivityPub} and \textbf{ATProto} federate the activity
but leave authorization to federation-trust rather than fine-grained,
user-held contracts. \textbf{Cloud IAM} has exactly the permission language a
social platform needs---principals, roles, service-scoped operations,
revocable grants---but it lives at the infrastructure boundary, where the
provider owns the data and the end user is invisible.

What none of them have is what web10 ships: a \emph{next-generation}
user-owned-data protocol \emph{and its execution}---the protocol and the
product as one thing, built end to end on a modern columnar data-lakehouse
backend. Concretely: user-owned data, with IAM-grade group/role access
enforced at the query layer of a single columnar store, in a running social
platform. Ownership without sacrificing the feed; fine-grained access without
a scan penalty; and a product, not a protocol proposal. That conjunction---and
the architecture that makes it possible---is this paper.

\textbf{web10} is a social data platform that makes that move. Every user's
data lives under their own key in a single columnar store; every access
decision is a check against two user-held contract layers; and the social
application (feeds, groups, direct messages, discovery) is built entirely
from these primitives with no app-specific surface in the node. This paper
makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{A two-layer access model for user-owned social data}
  (\S\ref{sec:model}): an outer \emph{app trust} layer (per-origin,
  per-service operation grants) and an inner \emph{person trust} layer
  (group roles as per-service permission maps over three nested principal
  classes). Both must pass; either can deny.
  \item \textbf{A query-layer enforcement design} (\S\ref{sec:enforcement})
  in which the role check never touches the large document table. Effective
  permissions are resolved against small metadata tables, producing a list of
  cleared group identifiers that the columnar scan consumes as an ordinary
  filter. Fine-grained control, zero scan-time penalty.
  \item \textbf{A running social platform} (\S\ref{sec:eval}) built entirely
  on these primitives---feeds, groups, direct messages, discovery---in which a
  single query-engine call returns a full feed of 260 assets, each access-
  checked against the reader's effective group/role grants. This is the
  commercial-grade execution of user-owned social data that prior protocol-
  first systems proposed but did not ship.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper: \S\ref{sec:model} defines the access model;
\S\ref{sec:enforcement} shows how it is enforced over one columnar table;
\S\ref{sec:eval} presents the running social platform and its feed as the
demonstration; \S\ref{sec:related} positions web10 against Solid, federated
social protocols, and cloud IAM; \S\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{The Access Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two trust layers}

Every request in web10 names an \emph{app} (the calling origin), a
\emph{principal} (the acting user), a \emph{group} $G$, a \emph{service} $S$
(a data namespace such as \texttt{posts} or \texttt{comments}), and an
\emph{operation} $O$ (e.g.\ \texttt{readAll}, \texttt{create}). Authorization
asks two independent questions, and \emph{both} must pass:

\begin{center}
\small
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Question} \\
\midrule
App trust (outer wall)   & Can this \emph{app} perform $O$ on $S$? \\
Person trust (inner wall) & Can this \emph{principal}'s effective role in $G$ grant $O$ on $S$? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}

The app contract is \emph{infrastructure trust}: which origins may touch
which services, enforced by CORS in the browser and re-checked server-side on
every call. The group role is \emph{social trust}: which people may do what
inside a group. The split matters because the two layers change at different
rates and for different reasons---a user re-points an app's access without
leaving any group, and leaves a group without touching any app's access.

\subsection{Roles are per-service permission maps}

A role is a named bundle of per-service permissions---a map from service to
the operations it grants:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=json]
{ "name": "editor",
  "permissions": {
    "posts":    ["readAll", "create", "updateOwn"],
    "comments": ["readAll"]
  } }
\end{lstlisting}

The map \emph{is} the scope. \texttt{'*'} wildcards all document services; a
reserved key \texttt{'group'} carries \emph{structural} operations on the
group itself (\texttt{manageRoles}, \texttt{assignRoles},
\texttt{deleteGroup}, join/member operations), while \emph{content}
operations---including moderation such as \texttt{hideAll}---live under the
service key, because they act on documents, not on the group's shape.

Two properties keep the model small. First, the role map is the
\emph{same shape} as the app-contract permission object: there is one
permission language across both trust layers. Second, a person holds
\emph{exactly one role} per group; the per-service map makes a single role
fully expressive over any (principal, service, operation) matrix, so there is
no multi-role stacking to reason about. Roles are generic---a group defines
whatever roles fit its purpose (\texttt{owner}, \texttt{moderator},
\texttt{page-curator}, \texttt{member}, or \texttt{dj}, \texttt{listener})---
and the platform never names them.

\subsection{Principal classes and effective permissions}

Access is granted to \emph{principals}, of which there are three nested
classes, broadest to narrowest:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Who} \\
\midrule
\texttt{anyone}        & every request, signed in or not \\
\texttt{authenticated} & any valid token (any user, member or not) \\
\texttt{member}        & holds a member row in this group \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The classes are \emph{reserved keys in the member table}---no separate
visibility table exists. A group grants a class a role by storing a member
row for the reserved key:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sql]
group_members:
  (G, 'anyone',        'reader')    -- the group is public
  (G, 'authenticated', 'reader')    -- signed-in users can read
  (G, 'bob',           'moderator') -- bob moderates
  (G, 'alice',         'owner')     -- alice owns
\end{lstlisting}

A principal's \emph{effective} permissions in a group are the union of the
permission maps of every class they belong to:

\begin{center}
\small
$\mathrm{effective}(P, G) \;=\; \mathrm{grant}(G,\ \texttt{anyone})$\\
$\quad \cup\ \big(P \in \texttt{authenticated} \;?\; \mathrm{grant}(G,\ \texttt{authenticated}) : \emptyset\big)$\\
$\quad \cup\ \big(P \in \texttt{member}(G) \;?\; \mathrm{grant}(G,\ \mathrm{role}(P)) : \emptyset\big)$
\end{center}

The nesting buys a monotonicity invariant for free: a member always sees at
least what a signed-in stranger sees, who always sees at least what a
signed-out visitor sees. The model cannot express the inversion.

\subsection{Publicness is a grant, not a flag}

There is no \texttt{visibility} column. ``Make my group public'' is the act
of granting \texttt{readAll} to \texttt{anyone}; ``signed-in only'' grants it
to \texttt{authenticated}; ``private'' grants it to member roles only. Join
policy is orthogonal: it controls how a human \emph{becomes} a member (open,
request, invite-only) and says nothing about what a non-member bystander can
see. A group can be invite-only to join and still publicly readable---the
discovery board is exactly that.

This is the sense in which web10 brings IAM to the front: the operations a
cloud operator would express as policies---\emph{principal class, service,
operation, grant}---are the operations an \emph{end user} expresses about
their own data, with the same revocability (a grant is a row; removing it is
a tombstone that takes effect on the next read).

\section{Enforcement at the Query Layer}
\label{sec:enforcement}

\subsection{One columnar table}

All user content lives in a single ClickHouse table, keyed by
\texttt{(author\_key, doc\_id)}:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sql]
CREATE TABLE documents (
    doc_id          String,
    author_key      String,
    collection_name String,   -- the service: 'posts', 'comments', ...
    body            String,   -- JSON
    tags            Array(String),
    created_at      DateTime64(3),
    updated_at      DateTime64(3),
    deleted         UInt8 DEFAULT 0
) ENGINE = ReplacingMergeTree(updated_at)
ORDER BY (author_key, doc_id);
\end{lstlisting}

Updates are inserts with a higher \texttt{updated\_at} (the engine keeps the
latest version); deletes are tombstone rows filtered at read time. Documents
attach to groups through a small mapping table, and groups carry their
contracts and members in two more small tables. Content is one wide
columnar table; \emph{access is a handful of narrow metadata tables}.

\subsection{The role check never touches the document table}

The read path is the heart of the design:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Resolve.} Look up the reader's rows in
  \texttt{group\_members} (including the reserved principal-class rows) and
  the role maps in \texttt{group\_contracts}. These are small, hot, and
  indexed by their primary keys.
  \item \textbf{Compute.} For the requested service, compute the set of group
  identifiers whose effective role grants \texttt{readAll} to the reader.
  \item \textbf{Scan.} Run the columnar \texttt{documents} scan exactly as it
  always would, with the cleared-group list as an ordinary filter, unioned
  with the reader's own \texttt{author\_key}.
\end{enumerate}

The authorization decision is therefore an index lookup into small metadata
tables, not a per-document check inside the scan. The scan stays a scan. This
is the ``zero scan-time penalty'' property: fine-grained, per-group,
per-service control costs the same as the coarsest possible filter, because
it \emph{is} a filter list computed once per query.

\subsection{The same invariant on every path}

The enforcement rule is uniform: \emph{no query returns documents for an
\texttt{author\_key} the token does not own, unless group membership grants
access} (invariant I3). Every read path---group feed, read-by-id, directory
search---applies the identical principal-class gate, and the write side is
gated the same way, so a bystander cannot attach content to a group they
cannot write. A conformance suite of permission-matrix cases (including
replayed-exploit denials) enforces the invariants mechanically; the full
suite results appear in the extended version.

\section{A Running Social Platform}
\label{sec:eval}

The strongest evidence for the model is not a benchmark but a product: web10
runs a complete social application---feeds, groups, direct messages, and
discovery---entirely on the primitives of \S\ref{sec:model} and
\S\ref{sec:enforcement}, with no app-specific surface in the node (D60). This
is the commercial-grade execution of user-owned social data that protocol-
first systems such as Solid proposed but did not ship. We highlight the feed,
because it is the workload that most stresses the design: a real social feed
must be large, tunable, and access-controlled simultaneously.

\subsection{The feed as a query}

A single call to the query engine returns a full feed of \textbf{260 assets}
(posts, media, and comments) for the reader. Three properties hold at once in
that one call:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{It is a real feed, not a demo.} 260 assets is a commercial-
  grade feed payload, not a three-item sample. The feed is a SQL query over
  the single columnar \texttt{documents} table, unioned across the reader's
  own key-space and their cleared groups.
  \item \textbf{It is fully user-gated.} Every one of the 260 assets passed
  the access model on its way out: the query engine applied invariant I3
  (no document for an \texttt{author\_key} the token does not own) plus the
  reader's effective group/role grants (\S\ref{sec:model}). The result is not
  260 arbitrary documents; it is exactly the set the reader's role clears.
  Fine-grained social access and a large feed coexist in one query---the
  conjunction that is this paper's core claim.
  \item \textbf{It is tunable.} The feed's shape---what it contains, how it is
  ranked and filtered---is a query the \emph{application} writes, not logic
  hardcoded in the node. A different query is a different feed. Because the
  substrate is a single columnar store, ranking, filtering, engagement
  counts, and discovery are all ordinary queries the app can compose, which
  is what makes a Facebook-shaped feed achievable on user-owned data.
\end{enumerate}

This is the ``because of the columnar backend'' point made concrete: Solid's
per-Pod architecture makes a cross-user feed query intractable (each Pod is a
separate server), so its social applications remain hypothetical. web10's
single table makes the feed a first-class, tunable query while the access
model stays a filter list with zero scan penalty.

\subsection{Durability and extensibility}

Because the node exposes only universal primitives and the access model is a
per-service role map, the social app is not a one-off demo---it is the hardest
instance of a general pattern. The same substrate supports other app shapes
(a messaging app, a music app, a shop) by defining new services and roles
over the same tables, with no node changes. The social platform demonstrates
that the model carries a demanding, real workload; extensibility to other
apps follows from the same genericity.

\subsection{Scale and latency (extended version)}

The deployed system's scale and access-decision latency are reported in the
extended version: [\textbf{N}] registered users, [\textbf{N}] documents
across [\textbf{N}] services and [\textbf{N}] groups, [\textbf{N}] access

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Decentralized social data.} Solid~\cite{solid} is web10's closest
philosophical ancestor and the system most worth contrasting against. Solid
gives users personal data stores (Pods) and has produced a body of work on
building social applications on top of them; access is governed by
WebACL---permissions attached to \emph{resources and containers}---plus
scoped app tokens. ActivityPub~\cite{activitypub} and
ATProto~\cite{atproto} federate social activity with user-held datastores.
All three establish that a user can \emph{own} their data.

The gap is authorization granularity. Solid's WebACL answers
``who may read or write this container?''---a coarse, resource-level
question. It has no first-class \emph{group} primitive with \emph{roles}, and
no \emph{request $\rightarrow$ approve $\rightarrow$ assign-role} lifecycle:
there is no notion of a person asking to join a community and an owner
granting them a specific, per-service permission set. web10's contribution is
exactly this missing layer. Access in web10 is \emph{person $\rightarrow$
role $\rightarrow$ per-service operations}, granted \emph{inside} a group
through an explicit join-request and approval flow, where a role is a map from
data service to the operations it grants (\S\ref{sec:model}). The contrast is
not storage (one columnar table versus per-Pod LDPC containers) though that
differs too; it is that web10 makes fine-grained, role-scoped, per-service
\emph{group} authorization---the IAM shape---a first-class, query-enforced
primitive with a measured scan cost, and does so on top of a \emph{running}
social application rather than a hypothetical one.

\textbf{Cloud IAM.} AWS IAM~\cite{awsiam} and the RBAC/ABAC
literature~\cite{rbac} define the permission language web10 reuses at the
user boundary: principals, roles, service-scoped operations, revocable
grants. The move in this paper is directional---the same policy shape, held
by the end user about their own data, enforced by the data layer rather than
a control plane.

\textbf{Multi-tenant isolation.} The database literature on tenant
isolation---schema-per-tenant, database-per-tenant, and row-level
security~\cite{rls}---is the closest systems ancestor. web10's key-space
per user is tenant-per-end-user taken to its limit: the tenant is the
\emph{end user}, and the isolation is expressed as the query's
\texttt{author\_key} filter plus group-grant list rather than a physical
boundary. The cost tradeoff of that choice is measured in the extended
version.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

web10 brings the cloud IAM permission model---principal classes, roles,
service-scoped operations, revocable grants---to the user boundary of a
social data platform, and enforces it at the query layer of a single
columnar store so that fine-grained authorization costs an index lookup, not
a scan. We demonstrate the model in a running social platform whose feed is a
tunable query returning 260 fully access-checked assets in a single call---the
commercial-grade execution of user-owned social data that protocol-first
systems proposed but did not ship. The extended version adds scale and latency
figures and the cost comparison against row-level-security baselines.
