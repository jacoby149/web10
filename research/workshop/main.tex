\begin{abstract}
Decentralized platforms distribute data across providers, but access control
often remains tied to each platform's identity system and application model.
We present \textbf{web10}, a user-addressed data platform that represents
content and access relationships in a shared columnar store. Applications
query service-namespaced documents through a uniform permission model in which
principals from different nodes can hold roles in user-managed groups. The
node enforces access at the query boundary by supplying reader-filtered
relations before joins and aggregations execute. Clients communicate directly
with the relevant nodes rather than relying on server-to-server content
federation. We describe the architecture and demonstrate cross-application
reuse: a game and a marketplace read social-written profiles without modifying
the social application or adding application-specific node endpoints.
Connected browser flows exercise consent and revocation.
An isolated two-node experiment exercises foreign-principal reads and local
grant revocation while reusing cached home-node certification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Email is the decentralized web's proof of concept. A global identifier
(\texttt{you@provider}), a sovereign store per provider, and standardized
server-to-server mail transport let you send a message across providers---no
central authority or shared store. Each provider's
server is independent; the protocol and the identifier are what make the
network work.

Email decentralized the \emph{data plane} for one format: mail. It did not
decentralize \emph{access}. Your contacts, your blocking, your sharing
permissions live inside one provider's identity system. You can email a Yahoo
user from Gmail, but you cannot grant that Yahoo user \emph{editor} on your
Google Calendar. The payload crossed the boundary; the permissions did not.
Every social platform inherits this split: the content can be portable in
principle, but access relationships remain locked to the platform's backend:
who follows whom, who belongs to a group, and who may read or contribute.

\textbf{web10} extends the email model in two directions at once. First, it
generalizes the data plane from one format to \emph{all} formats: a shared
columnar store in which any application defines its own services and queries,
so a post, a profile, a game score, and a marketplace listing are all documents
in the same store, addressed by the same primitives. Second, and more
consequentially, it decentralizes the \emph{access layer} itself: a uniform,
user-managed permission vocabulary in which a grant is a row naming a
\emph{globally addressable principal} (\texttt{provider/username}), a group, a
service, and an operation. Any principal on any node can hold any role in a
group. The user is the policy administrator for their own data, and that policy
reaches across the network.

The result is a system that combines properties prior work treats as a
tradeoff. Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} places the deployed systems and the
email model that web10 generalizes against five capabilities. The marks are
\checkmark\ (present), $\times$ (absent), or $\sim$ (partial / scoped).

\begin{table}[t]

system with all five.}
\label{tab:capabilities}
\centering
\scriptsize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.6cm}ccccc@{}}
\toprule

The pattern is the email split, repeated. Email decentralized where data
\emph{goes}---a global address, sovereign stores, inter-provider mail transport---but
not who may \emph{access} it: you can mail a Yahoo user from Gmail, but you
cannot grant that user \emph{editor} on your Calendar. The social platforms
inherit the same split at larger scale. Mastodon~\cite{activitypub2018} and
Bluesky~\cite{kleppmann2024} let you follow someone on another server and carry
your data in principle, but their authorization is platform-defined and
coarse-grained---follow/block and collection-level visibility, not per-principal
grants over application-defined services. Facebook scales the data plane to a
massive lakehouse but keeps the access model entirely inside its own platform.
Solid~\cite{solid2016} is the closest prior work: it decentralizes data
\emph{and} some access, with WAC group agents~\cite{wac2022} and ACP
client/issuer matchers~\cite{acp2022}. But its access model is
\emph{resource-centric}---ACLs on linked-data resources---rather than a uniform
role/grant vocabulary over services that applications define, and its cross-Pod
query cost depends on deployment. web10 claims the center: all five
capabilities at once, over a network that is user-addressed rather than
server-federated.

The platform is deployed. web10-social is a creator-oriented social application
---feeds, groups, direct messages, discovery, short- and long-form video,
music---built entirely on the generic node: its persistent data and
authentication use web10's document, group, query, and media interfaces, with
no separate social-data backend. The node also serves a game and a marketplace
that reuse the social application's profile documents through the same access
boundary, without a node change or an application-specific endpoint.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{A user-addressed network model}
  (\S\ref{sec:network}): a federation of sovereign nodes in which the user's
  client mediates cross-node reads, each node re-authorizes every read against
  a verified token, and the access model is globally addressable by
  construction---any principal on any node can hold any role in a group. The
  model generalizes email's architecture from one data format to all formats
  and from content routing to access control.
  \item \textbf{A reader-specific query boundary}
  (\S\ref{sec:enforcement}): application-written SQL over a shared columnar
  store, in which service references resolve to reader-filtered CTEs \emph{before}
  the caller's joins and aggregation. The access decision is made on the input,
  not patched onto the output. The implementation combines policy resolution,
  SQL validation, and boundary construction above a shared database account.
  \item \textbf{A deployed platform and connected composition}
  (\S\ref{sec:eval}, \S\ref{sec:interop-results}): a social application, a game,
  and a marketplace over the same node, with real browser consent, cross-author
  joins, and revocation exercised. We report the metadata fan-out cost of the
  access layer and the batching decision that addresses it, and exercise
  home-node-certified reads across two isolated nodes
  (\S\ref{sec:federation-results}).
\end{enumerate}

The evaluation in \S\ref{sec:method} reports the connected interoperability
checks and the metadata-resolution measurement. \S\ref{sec:related} relates the
design to decentralized storage, IAM, and multi-tenant databases;
\S\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{The Access Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two trust layers}

For a group-scoped content operation, the model distinguishes an \emph{app}, a
\emph{principal} (the acting user), a \emph{group} $G$, a \emph{service} $S$
(a data namespace such as \texttt{posts} or \texttt{comments}), and an
\emph{operation} $O$ (e.g.\ \texttt{readAll}, \texttt{create}). Authorization
asks two questions where the corresponding gates apply:

\begin{center}
\small
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Question} \\
\midrule
App trust (outer wall)   & Can this \emph{app} perform $O$ on $S$? \\
Person trust (inner wall) & Does membership or a class/role grant allow $O$ on $S$ in $G$? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}

The app contract expresses trust in a calling application. In the current
document and query paths, authenticated browser-style tokens are checked against
an origin-keyed contract when an HTTP \texttt{Origin} is present; without that
header, this app-contract check is skipped. Anonymous queries also skip it.
OAuth-delegated tokens instead select a \texttt{client\_id}-keyed contract,
including when no Origin is present. The node enables wildcard, non-credentialed
CORS; endpoint code enforces application grants separately. Origin identifies
the browser calling context, while the delegated token carries the OAuth client
identity. Non-browser clients control their own Origin headers. Identity
verification and document/group authorization are separate checks.

The group layer expresses trust in people. The split lets a user revoke an
app grant without leaving a group, or leave a group without changing app
grants.

\subsection{Roles are per-service permission maps}

A role is a named bundle of per-service permissions---a map from service to
the operations it grants:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=json]
{ "name": "editor",
  "permissions": {
    "posts":    ["readAll", "create", "updateOwn"],
    "comments": ["readAll"]
  } }
\end{lstlisting}

The map \emph{is} the scope. \texttt{'*'} wildcards all document services; a
reserved key \texttt{'group'} carries \emph{structural} operations on the
group itself (\texttt{manageRoles}, \texttt{assignRoles},
\texttt{deleteGroup}, join/member operations), while \emph{content}
operations---including moderation such as \texttt{hideAll}---live under the
service key, because they act on documents, not on the group's shape.

Two properties keep the model small. First, the role map is the
\emph{same shape} as the app-contract permission object: there is one
permission language across both trust layers. Second, a person holds
\emph{one explicit member role} per group; compound permissions can be placed
in that role rather than stacked through multiple assignments. Policies use
positive grants; explicit deny and context-dependent conditions are outside
this model. A group defines
whatever roles fit its purpose (\texttt{owner}, \texttt{moderator},
\texttt{page-curator}, \texttt{member}, or \texttt{dj}, \texttt{listener})---
and applications supply their role definitions. Some lifecycle operations use
conventional names such as \texttt{member}.

\subsection{Principal classes and effective permissions}

Access is granted to \emph{principals}, of which there are three nested
classes, broadest to narrowest:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Who} \\
\midrule
\texttt{anyone}        & every request, signed in or not \\
\texttt{authenticated} & a verified non-anonymous user, member or not \\
\texttt{member}        & holds a member row in this group \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The broad classes \texttt{anyone} and \texttt{authenticated} are represented by
\emph{reserved keys in the member table}; individual members use their own
principal keys. No separate visibility table is needed. For example:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sql]
group_members:
  (G, 'anyone',        'reader')    -- the group is public
  (G, 'authenticated', 'reader')    -- signed-in users can read
  (G, 'bob',           'moderator') -- bob moderates
  (G, 'alice',         'owner')     -- alice owns
\end{lstlisting}

A principal's \emph{effective} permissions in a group are the union of the
permission maps of every class they belong to:

\begin{center}
\small
$\mathrm{effective}(P, G) \;=\; \mathrm{grant}(G,\ \texttt{anyone})$\\
$\quad \cup\ \big(P \in \texttt{authenticated} \;?\; \mathrm{grant}(G,\ \texttt{authenticated}) : \emptyset\big)$\\
$\quad \cup\ \big(P \in \texttt{member}(G) \;?\; \mathrm{grant}(G,\ \mathrm{role}(P)) : \emptyset\big)$
\end{center}

The content gate combines this grant union with membership admission. In
\texttt{can\_read\_group} and
\texttt{can\_write\_group} helpers, an active member is admitted for content
reads and creates without requiring service-specific \texttt{readAll} or
\texttt{create} in that member's role. For non-members, the class grants supply
those permissions. Other role-controlled operations use their respective
permission checks.

The union is monotone across principal classes under otherwise equal
conditions. Blocks, sharing state, hidden documents, and node moderation apply
additional visibility restrictions to the resulting content.

\subsection{Publicness is a grant, not a flag}

There is no \texttt{visibility} column. ``Make my group public'' is the act
of granting \texttt{readAll} to \texttt{anyone}; ``signed-in only'' grants it
to \texttt{authenticated}; ``private'' withholds those non-member grants. Join
policy is orthogonal: it controls how a human \emph{becomes} a member (open,
request, invite-only) and says nothing about what a non-member bystander can
see. A group can be invite-only to join and still publicly readable---the
discovery board is exactly that.

This is the sense in which web10 brings IAM to the front: the operations a
cloud operator would express as policies---\emph{principal class, service,
operation, grant}---are the operations an \emph{end user} expresses about
their own data. A grant is represented by metadata rows and revoked through
tombstones. Subsequent checks use latest-row resolution. End-to-end revocation
latency comprises database visibility, concurrent checks, in-flight requests,
and the remaining lifetime of issued capabilities.

\section{A User-Addressed Network}
\label{sec:network}

The access model of \S\ref{sec:model} is defined for one node. This section
states the network model that makes it a federation rather than a single
deployment, and the property that distinguishes web10 from both email and from
server-federated systems like ActivityPub.

\subsection{Email's architecture, generalized}

Email combines globally addressed accounts (\texttt{you@provider}), sovereign
provider stores, standardized inter-provider mail transport, and no central
shared mailbox database. Its servers do exchange mail with other servers;
client-mediated cross-node reads are web10's transport choice, not a property
inherited from email.

web10 retains independent providers and global addressing. The identifier is
\texttt{provider/username}---the provider is the node, the username is the
user, and the pair is globally unique across the federation. The store is a
shared columnar data plane (one \texttt{documents} table per node, keyed by
\texttt{(author\_key, doc\_id)}), not a per-user mailbox, but it is still
sovereign: each node's store belongs to that node, and a user's home node is
where their account and their grants live.

The data and access extensions are: (1)~the data is no longer locked to one format---any
application defines its own services over the same store, so the network
carries posts, profiles, scores, listings, and whatever an app names; and
(2)~the \emph{access layer} is decentralized, not just the data layer. A grant
is a row \texttt{(group, principal, role)} in which the principal is the global
\texttt{provider/username}. A user on node A can be granted \texttt{editor} on
a group that lives on node B. The permission vocabulary of \S\ref{sec:model}
---principal class, service, operation, grant---is the same whether the
principal is local or three nodes away. Email let your data leave the building;
web10 lets your \emph{permissions} leave it too.

\subsection{The linchpin: verified, not registered}

A user's home node is where their account lives. When that user reads content
on another node, they do not sign up there. Their client presents their
home-node token; the foreign node obtains home-issuer certification, resolves the
principal to \texttt{provider/username}, and applies its own group-membership
and grant checks. The user's identity is \emph{verified, not registered}, on
the foreign node. Consequences:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item A user's following set can span $N$ nodes; they hold exactly one
  account, on their home node.
  \item A node's data is read only by holders of a verified token that the
  node's own membership and grant checks authorize---whether the token was
  minted by this node or a foreign one.
  \item ``Following someone on another node'' is a \emph{cross-node
  membership}: a member row on the foreign node referencing a foreign
  principal. The content never crosses the boundary; only the membership
  reference does.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The boundary: the HTTP API and a verified token, never the database}

A node never queries another node's store. The cross-node boundary is the
\emph{HTTP API} with a \emph{verified token}. A foreign node's API call passes
through that node's normal authentication and access gate, exactly like a local
call. We reject database-level cross-server mechanisms (ClickHouse's
\texttt{remote()}/\texttt{cluster()}) for federation: they are for a trusted
cluster under one operator, they couple schemas across independent nodes, and
they create a DB-level trust path that bypasses the API's authorization
entirely---a node operator's database credentials would become a federation
credential.

This yields an invariant that the whole model is built to hold:

\paragraph{I6.} \emph{The cross-node boundary is the HTTP API and a verified
token; remote data is content, never control.} A foreign node's response is
untrusted content---the same category as a post body. It is never query input,
never a key into the local store, and never a grant.

I6 is what defeats the obvious attack. A malicious node B cannot ``mine'' node
A during a cross-node read, because there is no cross-node \emph{query}: A never
reads B's store, and B's API responses reach A only as JSON content that A has
already authorized for this reader. The feed merge is a result-set \emph{union}
of independently authorized reads; B's data are values being merged, never
parameters to a query against A. A data reference is not a grant: if B's
content points the client at an A-node document, A re-authorizes that fetch
against the reader's token and denies it if the reader is not authorized. The
same property that stops a local user reading other posts (I3) stops a foreign
node from steering a read---federation does not weaken it, it adds the
verification step so the serving node can check a foreign token before applying
I3.

\subsection{The read model: client-side fan-out}

When a user's feed spans nodes, the \emph{client} makes the cross-node calls.
The client holds the user's home-node token, groups the user's following set by
provider, and queries only the distinct nodes the user follows people on---
targeted, not broadcast. Each node returns its own access-gated result set; the
client performs a ranked merge. No node proxies or caches another node's data,
so there is no node-to-node credential to steal and no stale-cache confusion.
The node remains a dumb broker (D60): it does not learn to ``merge feeds.''

\subsection{Domain as portable alias: the address survives provider changes}

The \texttt{provider/username} principal is the address, but the username need
not be an opaque string. Through the authenticator's claim flow, a user can
bind a DNS domain to their account: a TXT record at
\texttt{web10.\emph{domain}} carries
\texttt{web10.v=1;\allowbreak provider=\emph{node};\allowbreak user=\emph{username}}, and an
authenticated \texttt{POST /v3/domains/verify} request makes the node perform
the DNS lookup itself, checking that the record's provider matches the node
and its username matches the authenticated caller before storing the
verified alias. The record is a pointer, not a credential: DNS lookup is
discovery, not authentication, and the username remains the principal---
groups, tokens, and the read gate never key on the domain. The same pattern
the web already uses for identity claims (DKIM, SPF, \texttt{OpenPGPkey}),
applied to a social identity.

The practical consequence is portability in the strong sense: the domain is a
real, resolvable, user-owned identifier that survives provider changes by
construction. A user deplatformed by a mainstream platform can point their
domain at a sovereign node that will not remove their content, and the
followers who follow the domain resolve to the user's new location. The legal
distinction is specific: follower relationships are the \emph{user's} data,
not the platform's. A platform that deletes a user's follower list is deleting
the user's data---a different legal category than removing the platform's own
content. web10 makes this concrete: the following relationships live in the
user's sovereign data plane, addressed by the user's domain, and move with
them to a node that will not delete them.

The enforcement path makes this concrete in the read model: web10 apps are
implemented entirely in the UI, over the web10 API, with no platform backend
in the read path. When the social client renders a user's feed, it pulls
directly from the servers of the nodes the user is following---grouping the
following set by provider and querying each node directly with the user's
home-node token. The content arrives as authorized data, not as a
platform-mediated feed.

This is the internet-freedom property that the email model proved and the
social platforms lost: your address is yours, you can switch providers, and no
single platform can unilaterally erase your inbox or block your senders. web10
restores that property for social data, and the domain-as-alias mechanism makes
it concrete rather than conceptual.
\subsection{What is running and what is the active build}

The network model is the architecture; runtime maturity is phased. Foreign
readers use \texttt{\{provider\}/users/\{username\}}. Local writers and readers
retain legacy bare keys until a coordinated migration; canonicalization is
not complete across the identity layer. For a foreign token, the serving node
asks its home issuer's HTTPS \texttt{/certify} endpoint and caches a successful
identity answer for that exact JWT until expiry. The issuer verifies with its
own signing secret; the serving node still checks its own access grants on
every read. This live-certification design does not require JWKS or shared
signing secrets. The isolated experiment in \S\ref{sec:federation-results}
uses independently generated node keys and owner-managed foreign grants.
Production key provisioning, foreign self-join, complete principal migration
and client-side feed merging remain open. The current shared signing-key
default must not be mistaken for independently provisioned production nodes.

\section{Enforcement at the Query Layer}
\label{sec:enforcement}

\subsection{One columnar table}

A ranked feed has analytical components: joins across authors and engagement,
filtering, aggregation, and sorting. A social application also has point reads,
updates, and coordination-heavy operations. web10 chooses
ClickHouse~\cite{clickhouse2024} for
both structured content and operational metadata rather than maintaining a
separate Postgres-to-ClickHouse replication pipeline. User content occupies
one shared \texttt{documents} table keyed by \texttt{(author\_key, doc\_id)}.
The user namespace is logical isolation within that table, not a per-user
database or physical collection. Large media objects reside in object storage.
The following simplified schema omits reference and advertising fields:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{\columnwidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=sql]
CREATE TABLE documents (
    doc_id          String,
    author_key      String,
    collection_name String,   -- the service: 'posts', 'comments', ...
    body            String,   -- JSON
    tags            Array(String),
    created_at      DateTime64(3),
    updated_at      DateTime64(3),
    deleted         UInt8 DEFAULT 0
) ENGINE = ReplacingMergeTree(updated_at)
ORDER BY (author_key, doc_id);
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

Updates insert a newer version; deletes insert tombstones. Background merges
alone do not establish read correctness: reads must select the latest version
\emph{including tombstones}, then filter deleted rows. Filtering tombstones
before deduplication can resurrect the previous version. Documents attach to
groups through \texttt{doc\_groups}; \texttt{group\_contracts} and
\texttt{group\_members} hold contracts and assignments. These are narrower
metadata tables whose size grows with groups and memberships.

One structured store removes a cross-database synchronization dependency.
Its operational costs include version accumulation, merge pressure, read-time
deduplication, point-query latency, and shared-resource contention. Multi-row
content/membership changes and concurrent revocation require explicit
consistency handling. Backup and recovery span database metadata and object
storage; these are requirements of the design.

\subsection{The query boundary: a wall, not a membrane}

Application-written SQL over a multi-tenant store requires a boundary before
the caller's relational operations. The query API accepts a constrained
read-only SQL surface, not arbitrary database access. Its intended boundary is
a \emph{wall, not a membrane}: validated service references read reader-filtered
CTEs, rather than raw tables whose output is filtered afterward. Correctness
depends on validation coverage and SQL dialect interpretation, as discussed below.

\paragraph{Why output-side filtering is unsound.} The natural design wraps the
caller's query and filters the \emph{result}, e.g.\
\texttt{SELECT * FROM (q) WHERE <filter>}. This fails because the caller's
query has \emph{already read the raw table} inside the parentheses, and an
outer filter cannot undo an aggregation that baked everyone's data in:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=sql]
-- counts every user's comments; the outer group filter is too late
SELECT author_key, count(*)
FROM documents WHERE collection_name = 'comments'
GROUP BY author_key
\end{lstlisting}
The aggregate has already collapsed all authors into counts before any row-level
filter could run. Filtering the output of an aggregation is not access control.

\paragraph{Redirecting the input instead.} web10 parses submitted SQL to an
AST; it does not send that original string directly to ClickHouse. It validates
table references and injects a \emph{boundary CTE} under each referenced service
name. The CTE deduplicates the service's documents, joins current
\texttt{doc\_groups} mappings, filters to readable groups, and applies block,
sharing, hidden-document, and node-ban exclusions. Thus
\texttt{SELECT ... FROM posts} resolves to a service CTE before aggregation or
joins. The readable set can include public or authenticated-class grants, not
just the reader's memberships. An empty set produces an empty service CTE.
Before compilation, an authenticated request with explicit groups is rejected
with 403 if none of its referenced services has a readable group. The query
boundary does not automatically union in all reader-owned documents.

\paragraph{The walk and the backstop.} The compilation is five steps: (1)~parse
to an AST (unparseable or multi-statement input is rejected); (2)~check the
statement kind (\texttt{SELECT} and supported set operations, not
\texttt{INSERT}, \texttt{DROP}, etc.; this is not full SQL type checking);
(3)~walk every \texttt{Table} node, including inside caller CTEs and subqueries,
rejecting raw node tables, ungranted services, and table functions
(\texttt{file()}, \texttt{s3()}, \texttt{numbers()}, represented in the
validated parser shapes as empty-named \texttt{Table} nodes);
(4)~inject the needed boundary CTEs ahead of the caller's CTEs and re-emit from
the validated AST; and (5)~\emph{round-trip}---re-parse the compiled SQL and
require it to come back as exactly one statement. The round-trip checks
composition syntax. Targeted parser/engine tests exercise selected dialect and
name-resolution cases. The executed query is emitted from
the validated AST.

\paragraph{Why place policy above ClickHouse?} web10 uses a shared database
service account and constructs a reader-specific boundary combining membership,
class grants, and visibility exclusions. This design puts policy orchestration
in the application rather than provisioning a database account and policy for
each end user. PostgreSQL policies offer another enforcement placement, using
expressions, functions, and
subqueries to consult dynamic authorization state~\cite{postgres-rls}; an
equivalent membership policy provides a comparison baseline for the cost of
these enforcement placements.

\paragraph{Trusted implementation.} The parser, validator, generated CTEs,
identity resolution, metadata checks, and ClickHouse name-resolution semantics
are in the trusted computing base. The service account must read raw tables to
execute generated CTEs; its privileges do not independently enforce each
reader's boundary. Repository tests cover selected top-level, caller-CTE,
subquery, table-function, and aggregation cases, with a live-node E2E gauntlet
for supported shapes. Parser and engine upgrades require renewed
compatibility and adversarial testing.

\subsection{Metadata resolution and relational cost}

The read path is the heart of the design:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Resolve.} Take explicitly requested candidate groups, or
  discover the reader's memberships. Parse the query to identify its services.
  \item \textbf{Compute.} For each referenced service and candidate group,
  check active membership or resolve class grants through member and contract
  metadata. The current query endpoint calls \texttt{readable\_groups}, which
  loops over candidates; a check may issue several metadata queries.
  \item \textbf{Execute.} Inject each service's readable-group set into its
  boundary CTE. ClickHouse performs document/mapping deduplication, the mapping
  join, visibility exclusions, and the caller's joins, aggregates, and sorting.
\end{enumerate}

For $s$ referenced services and $g$ candidate groups, this path performs up to
$s g$ group-gate evaluations, with early membership success reducing work
within a check. The repository also has a batched helper used elsewhere; the
query endpoint currently uses the per-group helper. Role resolution does not
need content bodies, but its metadata I/O and the generated relational work
both contribute to authorization cost.

The document-to-group join can yield multiple rows for one document attached
to several readable groups. Applications must choose whether to retain those
associations or deduplicate before counting and pagination. CTE expansion,
join cardinalities, exclusions, and sorting can change database work and query
plans. Evaluation accounts for both metadata resolution and this relational
work.

\subsection{Isolation rule and regression coverage}

The intended isolation rule is that another author's document requires an
authorized group path, including public or authenticated-class grants
(invariant I3). The query path implements this through service CTEs.
Permission-matrix and exploit-regression tests exercise the compiler and
endpoint boundaries. CRUD, directory, group-attachment, preparation, and media
paths have separate test scopes; \S\ref{sec:limits} records the current coverage
and open boundaries.

\subsection{Document-linked media capabilities}

Structured documents refer to binary objects in MinIO/S3-compatible storage.
The intended linkage is document authorization followed by capability issuance:
presigned URLs let browsers fetch individual objects directly; HLS uses
short-lived signed manifests and segment capabilities, with document checks
on manifest access. Expiry limits capability lifetime but does not retract
bytes already fetched or guarantee immediate revocation.

The query prepare pass combines media resolution, HLS signing, advertising,
and avatar preparation. This keeps capability issuance next to result
preparation while object storage serves the media bytes. Its authorization
boundary includes key ownership, authorized cross-user references, result
projections, public thumbnails, and stale capabilities
(\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Data policy, not privacy}

web10 deliberately trusts the node operator with readable content. Search,
ranking, moderation, and audit functions operate on plaintext. Tokens and signed
capabilities protect application/user boundaries; terms govern operator conduct.
This trust model supports node-side querying without requiring default
end-to-end encryption.

Here \emph{user-owned} denotes logical authorship namespaces and user-controlled
grants. The operator retains physical access to hosted data, and legal remedies
depend on terms, jurisdiction, and enforceability. Usable export and exit are
broader product goals. The isolated cross-node read slice is demonstrated in
\S\ref{sec:federation-results}; interoperable provider migration and production
federation remain forward scope (\S\ref{sec:network}).

\subsection{Implementation boundary}

Python/FastAPI handles request validation, identity and permission orchestration,
SQLGlot parsing/compilation, serialization, and result preparation. ClickHouse's
native engine performs scans, joins, aggregation, filtering, and sorting.
The \artifactdir{api/app}{node API source} and
\artifactdir{sdk/src}{TypeScript SDK} are available alongside the
\artifactfile{api/app/v3/endpoints/query.py}{query endpoint} and
\artifactfile{api/app/v3/services/safe_query.py}{boundary compiler}.
The production API command configures four Gunicorn processes with Uvicorn
workers. This service/database division of work puts query execution in
ClickHouse and orchestration in Python. Profiling separates API CPU, database
CPU, I/O waits, and queueing to locate the costs of parsing, metadata round
trips, response serialization, and preparation.

\subsection{The feed as a query}

The application's \artifactfile{marketing/web10-social/src/data/feed.ts}{feed implementation}
builds a query over \texttt{posts}, \texttt{reactions},
\texttt{comments}, and \texttt{profile}. It chooses chronological or weighted
ranking, joins engagement aggregates and author profiles, and supplies cursor
conditions. The node resolves those service names through the boundary CTEs.
Preparation can attach advertising and resolve media, HLS manifests, and author
avatars on returned rows. This separates the application's feed definition
from a dedicated feed endpoint, while retaining explicit server-side
preparation machinery.

Three implementation observations define what the feed path exercises:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Page assembly.} For a nonempty page with post IDs,
  \texttt{readFeedPage} issues the main query, then two engagement queries
  (reactions and comments) and a \texttt{contentViews} call. Group discovery
  precedes this work, and media bytes require separate fetches. UI-level
  reaction-state reads and other screen activity may add calls. Page assembly
  therefore combines one main composition query with scoped follow-up reads.
  \item \textbf{Authorization scope affects semantics.} The main query's
  engagement joins use the feed-group scope, while engagement documents may
  live in the discovery group. The follow-up queries use the union of feed
  groups and discovery to populate displayed counts. Counts are relative to
  readable relations, not necessarily global totals. The displayed counts
  can therefore differ from the counts used by the main ranking query.
  \item \textbf{The query is application-defined.} Ranking and filtering can
  change without adding a dedicated node feed endpoint. Applications can select
  their ranking and projections while reusing the same boundary construction
  and preparation services.
\end{enumerate}

Evaluation will measure both the complete page-assembly path and its main query,
recording client-call counts, internal metadata queries, and database work
separately alongside latency and throughput.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=sql]
WITH latest_profiles AS (
  SELECT author_key, body FROM profile
  QUALIFY row_number() OVER (
    PARTITION BY author_key
    ORDER BY updated_at DESC, created_at DESC,
             doc_id DESC) = 1
)
SELECT s.author_key,
       JSONExtractInt(s.body, 'score') AS score,
       p.body AS profile_body
FROM snake_scores s LEFT JOIN latest_profiles p
  ON s.author_key = p.author_key
ORDER BY score DESC LIMIT 50
\end{lstlisting}

profile group. This outer join can retain a score without a readable profile.

corresponding inner-join exclusion while the score records remain readable.
Applications therefore compose authorized relations, not unrestricted copies
of another application's data.

\subsection{Durability and extensibility}

Application-named services let different clients interpret documents without
changing the shared document schema. A conversation can be a group with message
documents; a music library can define track services and listener roles; a
shop can define catalog services. Domain-specific transactions, payment
workflows, search indexes, schema evolution, and external integrations can
extend this common substrate.

The durable separation is between stored content/access relationships and a
particular frontend. Replacing a frontend can reuse those primitives when it
understands their semantics. The user-addressed network model of
\S\ref{sec:network} is the architectural answer to provider migration: the
user's identity and grants are globally addressable, so moving providers is a
matter of the cross-node read and membership path completing, not a redesign.
That path is the active build (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:method}

The evaluation has four parts: a connected interoperability demonstration
with real browser consent and revocation, an isolated two-node correctness
scenario, a measurement of metadata fan-out cost, and a deployed operating
point. The first three use synthetic data on isolated test stacks; the
operating point is a read-only observation of the production node. None is a
capacity or scale result.

\subsection{Connected interoperability}
\label{sec:interop-results}

\textbf{Browser reuse.} A fresh account logged into web10-social through its
authenticator popup and edited its profile name to Alex Morgan. The browser
approved Snake's app request, earned and saved ten points, and displayed the
joined profile. After app-contract revocation, a new query returned 403 and a
leaderboard refresh cleared the result and displayed an access error. After
sign-out, Marketplace requested its service permission through the popup,
published a listing, and displayed the same profile. Reloading the listing's
detail URL preserved that result. API assertions checked exact authors, row
counts, profile bodies and listing identity, so the username fallback alone
could not satisfy the test.

\textbf{Independent access controls.} A separate HTTP case used three accounts
and two social-written profiles. It checked exact two-author score/profile and
listing/profile joins. With no app permission for \texttt{profile}, both queries
were denied; granting it enabled the joins, and revoking the app contract denied
them again. A separately labeled private-profile fixture isolated the group
gate: membership enabled an exact inner join; removal emptied it while the
scores remained readable. The fixture did not replace the social-written
profiles in the reuse checks. HTTP contracts were installed through the API;
the browser sequence above exercised the actual consent UI.

All three cases---desktop at 1440 pixels, mobile at 375 pixels, and HTTP
correctness---passed in the final fresh-stack run on 2 November 2026, with
zero retries or skips. Six flow captures and two identity-card captures per
viewport were retained and inspected. Iteration exposed a Marketplace race:
the initial listing read could overwrite a successful publication's status.
Keeping publication disabled until that read settled repaired it; a focused
regression failed before the repair and passed afterward.

\textbf{Provenance and scope.} The run used revision \texttt{db897bccb} plus a
retained patch/source overlay on a four-vCPU, 8-GB DigitalOcean sfo2 host, with
ClickHouse 24.8.14.39, Playwright 1.63.0 and Chromium 153. The artifact contains
source/config hashes, image identities, results and all 16 original captures.
The API, authenticator and social frontend used development configurations;
the marketing frontend was built. Schema initialization preceded API startup,
avoiding the inherited fresh-stack ordering failure. Project storage and the
droplet were removed after collection.

The hosted apps share one browser origin, although the HTTP case supplies a
separate app Origin. They demonstrate reuse across applications, not separate
browser-origin isolation or an independently recruited developer study. Clients
know the profile format; automatic schema discovery is outside this result.
Initial-letter avatars are not media-delivery evidence. These controls establish
named grant/revocation behavior, not a stale-access or capability-expiry bound.

\subsection{Cross-node identity and local revocation}
\label{sec:federation-results}

We ran a synthetic correctness scenario across two API processes, separate
ClickHouse and MinIO instances, independently generated HS256 signing secrets,
and real HTTPS home-issuer certification with an ephemeral test CA. Bob creates
a private group and document through B's API. Alice's A-issued JWT initially
fails both B's document read and query; giving a distinct B-side Alice the
membership also fails to authorize A's Alice. Bob then grants the exact foreign
principal through the owner API. Both read paths return the exact document;
removing that membership denies both, and regrant restores both.

All steps reuse the same JWT. A's TLS proxy records exactly one successful
certification across this lifecycle: B rechecks access while retaining cached
identity. A foreign-provider JWT signed with B's key is rejected twice, with
one failed issuer call and a negatively cached second rejection. An independent
Origin-bearing query gate denies a local namesake's app grant, allows a
qualified foreign app-contract fixture, and denies again after its revocation;
direct group-authorized reads remain available.

This is one completed isolated run, not a deployment or load result. The first
attempt failed on a harness import path before scenario execution; the repaired
run uses source revision \texttt{04da544442c3} plus the retained owner-list
repair patch (PR~\#1360), with no scenario retries. Docker-internal,
globally classified addresses exercise the unchanged public-address guard;
the test CA and network do not establish public-internet interoperability.
Foreign app grant/revocation use labelled service-function fixtures, not browser
consent or completed foreign enrollment. Owner grant/removal is not foreign
self-join. Receipts, executable source, image identities and verified cleanup
are retained in \artifactdir{research/federation-demo}{the experiment artifact}.

\subsection{The metadata fan-out cost of the access layer}
\label{sec:prelim}

The access layer resolves the reader's readable groups before the query
executes. We measured that cost to understand the design, not to report a
latency distribution: the question was whether per-group metadata resolution
scales with the number of candidate groups, and what a batched resolution
buys. Table~\ref{tab:fanout} reports one sequential trial on a DigitalOcean sfo2
\texttt{s-4vcpu-8gb} host (four vCPUs, 8\,GB nominal RAM, Ubuntu 24.04,
Linux 6.8, Docker 29.1.3), co-locating a single Uvicorn process and ClickHouse
24.3.18.7. The synthetic seed created 1,000 creators with one small post each
and a reader who joined all their groups; those groups also granted public
reads. Personal queries supplied $K=10$, $100$, or $1000$ candidate groups
on this same dataset and host. A separate discover query supplied one board
group ($K=1$), containing ten additional posts. Queries
selected document IDs and bodies, ordered by creation time and ID, with a
limit of 20. They did not execute engagement joins, media preparation or browser
page assembly. HTTP requests supplied no Origin header, so this trial does not
exercise the browser-origin app-contract gate.

\textbf{Variants.} The per-group resolver performs point metadata queries for
each candidate group. The batched resolver fetches memberships, principal-class
grants and role definitions across the candidate set in three database queries,
then resolves access in memory. This reduces round trips, not the amount of
permission metadata to process. We measured each resolver with real document
execution (B) and a fixed 20-row synthetic scan result (C). C retains the
policy/compilation path but is not an output-equivalent query execution.
A third variant (A) precompiles the known-readable projection and times
ClickHouse execution directly, outside the HTTP handler.

\textbf{Finding.} The per-group resolver's cost grows linearly with the number
of candidate groups: increasing $K$ from 10 to 1,000 increased median readable
time by $100.44\times$. The batched resolver, which fetches memberships,
principal-class grants, and role definitions in three queries and resolves in
memory, grows $9.12\times$ over the same range---and at $K=1000$ reduces median
readable time by $35.22\times$ and median handler time by $21.04\times$ relative
to per-group. The per-group path still completes requests, but its 7.64-second
median at $K=1000$ is a practical bottleneck for a reader who follows many
creators; the batched path's 363\,ms is not. This is the design decision the
measurement drives: the query endpoint should use the batched resolver for
large candidate sets.

The tradeoff is honest in both directions. Batching is not uniformly cheaper:
at the discover board's $K=1$, the per-group path is faster (9.53\,ms readable
versus 25.37\,ms batched), because three fixed round trips dominate when there
is one group to check. And batching does not make the cost constant: the batched
handler median still grew $4.47\times$ over the 100-fold increase in $K$, as the
metadata to process and the compiled query to emit both grow with the candidate
set. The result is a characterization of the access layer's cost structure---
metadata fan-out is the dominant term at large $K$, and batching is the
mitigation---not a claim of constant latency or a performance benchmark.

The trial is a single sequential run (20 samples per cell, 400 measurements,
one host, localhost HTTP, no concurrency, no warm-up exclusion), and the query
is a simplified projection without engagement joins, media preparation, or
browser page assembly. It measures the access layer's cost structure on a
representative workload; it does not establish a latency distribution, a
capacity bound, or a causal authorization-overhead estimate. The direct-scan
baseline (A) excludes authentication, compilation, and serialization, so
$B - A$ is a pipeline-versus-scan comparison, not a matched causal measurement.
Provenance: source revision \texttt{1a983520e}, seed 42, ClickHouse 24.3.18.7,
single Uvicorn process; an earlier 100-creator trial on a different host and
snapshot showed the same qualitative tradeoff and is not pooled with this table.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:limits}

The evaluation above is bounded by construction, and the open work is specific.

\textbf{Federation beyond the correctness slice.} The isolated experiment in
\S\ref{sec:federation-results} exercises home-issuer certification and
owner-managed foreign grant/removal, not a complete federated product.
Independent production signing-key provisioning, complete local-principal
migration, foreign self-join and app enrollment, multiworker/restart behavior,
public deployment and client-side feed merging remain open. Cached identity
answers intentionally last until JWT expiry; this does not establish immediate
issuer-side revocation or revoke already-issued media capabilities. JWKS would
be an optional verification optimization, not a prerequisite for the live-call
model.

\textbf{Workload and baseline.} The metadata fan-out measurement is a single
sequential trial on a simplified query. Full-feed latency (including engagement
joins, media preparation, and browser page assembly), concurrent capacity,
mixed allowed/denied access, and a matched shared-table policy baseline
(PostgreSQL with row-level security, same data and access semantics) remain to
be measured. A causal authorization-overhead estimate requires an isolated
ablation with equivalent preauthorized result sets, not the pipeline-versus-
scan comparison reported here.

\textbf{Correctness.} Security evidence consists of regression tests and the
named connected controls. Whole-system conformance, revocation-latency bounds
(racing grant changes with reads through metadata visibility, in-flight
requests, and issued capabilities), and the media capability boundary remain
open. Direct read-URL refresh enforces the verified caller's exact owner
namespace; that check alone does not establish stored-reference provenance or
revoke previously issued capabilities. Parser/engine semantic differences and result-projection provenance
require targeted testing.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The contribution is a \emph{combination}, not a single invention. Each
capability in Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} has prior art; the gap is that no
prior system combines all five---user-owned data in any format, user-managed
fine-grained access, access that is cross-node-addressable, composable
application-defined query, and a user-addressed network---in one system. We
position web10 against the closest work in each dimension and state the
specific gap each leaves.

\textbf{Decentralized social data.}
ActivityPub~\cite{activitypub2018} (Mastodon) defines client/server and
federated server/server activity delivery; its authorization is
platform-defined---follow/block and collection-level visibility---not
per-principal grants over application-defined services. ATProto~\cite{kleppmann2024}
(Bluesky) separates repositories, relays, and AppViews, supporting user agency
and provider switching; its visibility model is likewise collection-level, with
AppViews providing application-defined \emph{reads} but not user-managed
\emph{grants}. Both decentralize the data plane; neither decentralizes the
access layer. web10's distinction is directional: the access model is
user-managed and service-scoped, and the query is application-defined, not
platform-defined.

\textbf{Solid: the closest prior work.}
Solid~\cite{solid2016} separates applications from personal data stores (Pods),
with social application work~\cite{solid-apps2016} and a maintained Community
Solid Server~\cite{css2024}. Its access model is the most developed of any
decentralized social system: WAC supports resource permissions and group agents
through \texttt{acl:agentGroup}~\cite{wac2022}, and ACP adds agent, client, and
issuer matching with allow/deny mode sets~\cite{acp2022}. web10 does not claim
Solid lacks group or client authorization; it has both. The distinction is in
the \emph{shape} of the model. Solid's access control is \emph{resource-centric}
---ACLs attached to linked-data resources and containers, evaluated per
resource. web10's is \emph{service-centric} ---a uniform role/grant vocabulary
(principal class $\times$ service $\times$ operation) in which a role is a
per-service permission map, the same shape across the app-trust and
person-trust layers, and the grant is a row naming a globally addressable
principal. Concretely: Solid answers ``who may read or write this
\emph{resource}?''; web10 answers ``what \emph{operations} may this
\emph{principal class} perform on this \emph{service}, in this \emph{group},
under this \emph{app contract}?'' The latter is the IAM policy shape
(principal, service, operation, grant) exposed to the end user, not an
infrastructure operator. A second distinction is the query surface: Solid's
cross-Pod query cost depends on deployment, caching, and linked-data traversal
strategy; web10 composes application-defined SQL over a shared columnar store
with the access decision made on the input (\S\ref{sec:enforcement}). A
performance comparison would require a matched workload and deployment; we do
not claim one here.

\textbf{Cloud IAM and relationship-based authorization.}
AWS IAM (documentation)~\cite{awsiam} and the RBAC/ABAC
literature~\cite{ferraiolo1992, sandhu1996, abac2014} provide the policy
concepts web10 adapts at the user boundary: principals, roles, service-scoped
operations, revocable grants. The move is directional---a familiar policy
vocabulary exposed to \emph{users} about their own content and groups,
orchestrated by the API and query layer, rather than to infrastructure
operators about cloud resources. At industrial scale, Zanzibar~\cite{zanzibar2019}
is the closest prior art in \emph{relationship-based} authorization: a uniform
ACL data model and configuration language across hundreds of services, with
consistency as a first-class problem (causal ordering of ACL updates, snapshot
reads). Zanzibar's model is \emph{infrastructure-side}---the operator defines
the relationships and the service enforces them; the end user is not the policy
administrator. web10 inverts the boundary: the \emph{user} defines the grants,
the \emph{principal is globally addressable across nodes}, and the enforcement
is at the query layer over a shared columnar store. Zanzibar's consistency
guarantees motivate the explicit revocation-visibility evaluation we leave as
forward scope (\S\ref{sec:limits}); we do not claim equivalent consistency
here. TAO~\cite{tao2013} provides the deployed precedent for \emph{read-time}
privacy checking at social-graph scale (a billion reads/sec, privacy check per
item, pull not push); web10's query boundary is the same idea---every row
access-checked on its way out---applied to service-namespaced documents in a
columnar store with the check expressed as a reader-filtered CTE rather than a
per-item predicate.

\textbf{Multi-tenant isolation and row-level security.}
The database literature on tenant isolation---shared tables with an
organization identifier (Force.com~\cite{force2009}), schema-mapping
techniques~\cite{aulbach2008}, and the shared-versus-separate
spectrum~\cite{jacobs2007}---is the closest systems ancestor. web10's
key-space per user makes the \emph{end user} the logical tenant, taking the
spectrum to its limit: not one tenant per organization, but one logical tenant
per end user, in a shared columnar store. Authorship uses
\texttt{author\_key}; shared reads use authorized group relations rather than
a physical database boundary. PostgreSQL row-security
policies~\cite{postgres-rls} are the alternative enforcement location: per-row
policy evaluation inside the database engine. web10 places enforcement above
the engine---the reader-filtered CTE is constructed in the application layer
before the query reaches ClickHouse~\cite{clickhouse2024}---which is what makes
the access decision composable with application-defined joins and aggregation.
A matched policy and workload comparison (\S\ref{sec:method}) can distinguish
the costs of these placements; we report the design difference here and leave
the cost comparison as forward scope.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

web10 extends the email model---global identifiers, sovereign stores,
client-mediated communication, no central authority---from one data format to
all formats, and from content routing to access control. Content and access
relationships live in a shared columnar data plane; the query layer supplies
the reader's service relations before joins and aggregation; and the access
model is globally addressable, so a user's grants reach across the nodes. The
deployed social application, and the game and marketplace that reuse its
profiles, show the separation working: ranked feeds and scoped engagement
reads with query definitions in the application, and cross-app composition
without a node change.

The connected game and marketplace extend that separation beyond the social
interface: each joins its own records to social-written profiles without a new
node endpoint or a change to the social application. Browser consent and
cross-author app/group controls make this a demonstrated composition path,
rather than a hypothetical extension to other application domains.

The metadata fan-out measurement identifies per-group resolution as the access
layer's dominant cost at scale: at 1,000 candidate groups, batching reduced
median handler time from 7.64\,s to 363\,ms, while costing more at one group.
This is a design decision, not a scalability claim---and it is the kind of
concrete, measured tradeoff that an operating system earns. The forward scope
is specific: federation beyond the isolated correctness slice
(\S\ref{sec:federation-results}), full-feed and concurrent workload measurements, a
matched shared-table policy baseline, and revocation-latency bounds. The
architectural contribution is the integration itself: a user-addressed network
in which applications compose social views over reader-specific service
relations in a shared columnar store, and the user's access policy reaches
across the nodes.
